title: The Sun Analogy in the Republic
---
I wrote a description of all three images in the Republic to the best of my understanding while I was reading the Republic for the first time. The goal of the first image (the sun analogy, 507a-509c) is to describe a so called "child of goodness": since Socrates claims he cannot describe goodness directly, he instead opts to describe something that is analogous to goodness, which lies in the intelligible realm, to something in the visible realm. 
My description for the first image is given below: 
> In the visible realm, unlike sound, in order for the eyes to see objects, one needs light. Light is what links objects that are seen to the eyes. The source of light is the sun, which allows one to see, but it isn't sight itself. In much the same way, in the intelligible realm, goodness it what is responsible for knowledge. Socrates says: “When [the soul] focuses on something
illuminated by truth and what is, it understands, knows, and apparently possesses understanding, but when it focuses on what is mixed with obscurity, on what comes to be and passes away, it opines and is dimmed,
changes its opinions this way and that, and seems bereft of understanding." So when something is "lit up" by the sun, or posseses goodness, it can be known by the knower. Conversely, if it is not lit up, the soul does not have knowledge of the object, the soul only has beliefs of it. The sun also provides growth and nourishment just like how goodness provides the “reality” and the “being” of things, in addition to knowledge. 

I think what I don't fully understand is how goodness plays a role in making a particular more or less ideal. Are we to assume that if a particular possesses more goodness, than it is more ideal or perfect? Based on 508e-509a, the answer to this is in the affirmative, because the form of goodness, which has more goodness compared to the "good-like" forms of knowledge and truth, is also more "beautiful", which hear I am assuming means that it is a perfect example of goodness. So any form will have more goodness than particulars of the same type, and thus be more beautiful or ideal.  

Plato is exploiting the semantics of the word 'good' to also imply that a 'good' particular is 'good' at what it is intended to do.
