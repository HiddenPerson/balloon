The line is divided into four parts, which for simplicity I will label A, B, C and D. A and B are in the physical realm, and C and D are in the intelligible realm. A is to B what C is to D. A consists of likeness, so things like shadows, reflections on the surface of water, etc. B consists of all actual objects in the physical world. 

Both C and D are thought processes to eventually reach a conclusion. For C, 

> “the soul, using as images the things that were imitated before, is forced to investigate from hypotheses, proceeding not to a first principle but to a conclusion.” (510b)

So, in C things need to be taken for granted in order to reach a conclusion through thought. By contrast, in D:

>  “[the soul] makes its way to a first principle that is not a hypothesis, proceeding from a hypothesis but without the images used in the previous subsection, using forms themselves and making its investigation through them.” (510b) 

So, for D you first needs to take things for granted with one's senses until you find a ‘first principle’ so that you don’t need to depend on images in order to reach the conclusion. In particular, once this first principle is found, you make no use of things perceptible by the senses, i.e.,  “comes down to a conclusion without making use of anything visible at all, but only of forms themselves, moving on from forms to forms, and ending in forms.” (511c). However, in C one always depends on the senses on the journey, even if only thought is used to reach the conclusion. The important thing to note though is that if you take things for granted, you don’t have maximal understanding. 

One tricky part to understand is the difference between C and D through example. My only idea is based on this part in 510c: 
> "You’ll understand it more easily after the following preamble. I think you know that students of geometry, calculation, and the like hypothesize the odd and the even, the various figures, the three kinds of angles, and other things akin to these in each of their investigations, as if they knew them." 

I am not really familiar with Ancient Greek geometry, but what I believe is being said is that the objects of C are thought processes that depend on so-called hypotheses, which I am interpreting as other objects or properties that the Greeks at the time only knew how to define using images (or by referring to particulars in the physical world perhaps), and thus, they are taking these hypotheses for granted to reach a conclusion. Or, worded more simply, they are objects that we cannot without describing images, whereas the objects of D can be possibly be described only using forms. 

Something that bothers me about my understanding of the line is that it seems that the subsections A and B are sets of objects whereas the C and D are sets of thought processes. Even though they are all sets of different things, they are all still sets. However, I am not even sure if it makes senses to think of these subsections as sets.
