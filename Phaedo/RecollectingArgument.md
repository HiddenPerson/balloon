---

When reading the Phaedo for the first time, my first attempt of paraphrasing the recollection argument in Phaedo (72e-77d) goes as follows (with using the form of equality as an example): 
> The justification for the claim that the soul is immortal is that 'equal' objects in the physical world do not actually perfectly exemplify equality (since objects in the physical world might be slightly different from each other, or change overtime). Only equality itself perfectly exemplifies equality. Therefore, we cannot learn what equality itself is through our senses by observing physical objects. So how do we know what equality itself is? The only way to explain this is that we already knew it once because the soul in our body had the knowledge before our birth, and the imperfect 'equal objects' we then observe reminded us of what equality actually is. 

However, upon reading [Plato’s Middle Period Metaphysics and Epistemology](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/plato-metaphysics/) in the Stanford Encylopedia of Philosophy, I learned that Plato is not necessarily claiming that that equal objects in the physical world are approximations of equality, just that <span style="color:red"> unequal things can sometimes appear unequal</span>. To make that further assumption that particulars are imperfect examples of the perfect forms is the _approximationist_ viewpoint.  

***
My lingering doubt, which I highlighted in red above, pertains to 74b7-9. Based on the English translation that I read, I actually misread it twice: 
> Do not equal stones and sticks sometimes, while remaining the same, appear to one to be equal and to another to be
unequal?

but my interpretation is correct if I instead use the French, German and Italian translations: 
> Don’t equal stones and sticks sometimes,while being the same ones, seem at one
time equal, at another time not?

An argument posed to favor this translation over the English translation as posed in [^1] partly relies on knowledge of Ancient Greek that I cannot judge, and the fact that later on Socrates asks Simmias if there is ever a time when equality itself appeared to be inequality. This question would not make sense with the English translation because it is referring to the temporal state of equality, not whether equality appears different to different people.  

 I also agree with this argument because earlier in recollecting argument, the one characteristic that is given to distinguish between the forms and other physical objects is that they do not change over time.

[^1]: Ebrey, D. _Plato's Phaedo: Forms, Death and the Philosophical Life_, Cambridge: Cambridge University Press
